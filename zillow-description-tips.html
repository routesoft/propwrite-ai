What to say when every house has a 'chef's kitchen'

Zillow descriptions live or die on differentiation. Here's how to describe a kitchen, a yard, or a neighborhood that doesn't sound like every other one.

August 26, 2026 · 6 min read

Search "chef's kitchen" on Zillow in almost any mid-size city and the results run into the thousands. It's not that every one of those kitchens is lying — plenty genuinely have a six-burner range and a big island. It's that the phrase has been used so often it no longer tells a buyer anything distinguishing. It's become wallpaper.

The same thing has happened to a short list of other phrases: "entertainer's dream," "resort-style backyard," "prime location." Each one started as a real description of a real feature and has been worn smooth by repetition. The fix isn't to avoid the feature — it's to describe the specific version of it that's actually in this house.

Replace the label with the evidence

A buyer trusts a fact more than an adjective, because a fact can be checked against the photos. Instead of naming the category ("chef's kitchen"), name the thing that would make a buyer conclude it themselves.

Overused phrase Try instead
Chef's kitchen "Six-burner gas range, waterfall-edge island, pantry big enough to hide the mixer"
Resort-style backyard "Pool with a baja shelf, outdoor kitchen under a covered patio, no rear neighbors"
Prime location "Six-minute walk to the Elm Street Caltrain stop, two blocks from the Saturday farmers market"
Entertainer's dream "Open kitchen-to-living flow, bar seating for six, sliding doors to a covered deck"

Zillow rewards descriptions that match what the photos show

Buyers on Zillow flip through photos and read the description almost simultaneously, often on a phone, often comparing to four other tabs. A description that oversells relative to the photos creates a small, immediate letdown — and buyers remember which listings did that. A description that names exactly what's visible builds a bit of trust that carries into the showing request.

If a phrase would survive being pasted into a listing with completely different photos, it isn't describing this house.

Don't over-correct into a spec sheet

The goal isn't to strip out all feeling and list dimensions like an inspection report. It's to earn the feeling with a fact instead of asserting it with an adjective. "Morning light floods the kitchen through the east-facing bay window" does more work than either "sunny kitchen" (vague) or "kitchen has a bay window" (flat) alone — it keeps the feeling and grounds it in something real.

Build a short list of house-specific facts before you write a word

Before drafting, jot five or six details that are true of this house and would be false of the listing next door: an exact appliance brand, a renovation year, a specific view, a distance to something buyers in this area actually care about. Those five or six facts are the raw material — the description is just arranging them so a buyer keeps reading.
