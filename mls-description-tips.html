How to write an MLS description that actually gets clicks

Buyers scan MLS descriptions in seconds. Here's the structure, the words to cut, and the details that actually earn a showing request.

August 15, 2026 · 7 min read

Most MLS descriptions lose the buyer in the first sentence. Not because the property is wrong for them — because the writing gives them nothing to picture. "Welcome to this stunning home" tells a scrolling buyer exactly nothing they didn't already know from the thumbnail.

Buyers on Zillow and the MLS spend, on average, a handful of seconds deciding whether to open a listing before moving to the next one. The description's job isn't to sell the house. It's to earn the click into the photos.

Lead with the detail that isn't in every other listing

Every listing in a price range has "updated kitchen" and "spacious backyard." Those aren't wrong, but they're not why this house gets a showing over the one three doors down. Open with whatever is actually specific: the year the roof was replaced, the direction the primary bedroom faces, the walk time to the elementary school, the fact that the kitchen was redone last spring and nothing has touched it since.

If a sentence could be pasted into any other listing on the same street without sounding wrong, it isn't doing any work.

Cut the words that have stopped meaning anything

"Cozy," "charming," and "must-see" have been used so often that buyers read past them without registering a single detail. They also carry unintended signals — "cozy" reads as "small" to a lot of buyers, whether or not that's what you meant. Replace the adjective with the fact that would have made a buyer believe it: not "cozy living room," but "living room fits a sectional and still leaves the doorway clear."

A quick swap list

  • Instead of "spacious" — give the square footage or a comparison ("primary bedroom fits a king bed with room for a reading chair")
  • Instead of "move-in ready" — name what was actually done and when ("new HVAC in 2025, repainted throughout in 2026")
  • Instead of "great for entertaining" — describe the layout that makes it true ("kitchen island seats four and opens directly to the patio")

Structure it the way a buyer actually reads

Buyers scan in a rough order: what is it, where is it, what's the standout, what's the catch (if any), what's next. A description that wanders — kitchen, then neighborhood, then back to the bathroom, then the school district — forces the reader to reassemble it themselves. Most won't bother.

A dependable order: one line on the property type and specs, two to three sentences on the two or three features that actually differentiate it, one line on location context that matters to the target buyer (commute, schools, walkability), and a short closing line that names the next step.

Match the length to the price point, not a template

A starter condo doesn't need six paragraphs, and a luxury estate undersold in eighty words looks like a mistake, not restraint. As a rough guide: entry-level and investment properties read well at 100–150 words, typical single-family homes at 150–220, and luxury or unusual properties can run to 250–300 when there's genuinely more to say. Past that, buyers stop reading before they finish.

Write the closing line like it's the only one that matters

It might be. A description that trails off after the feature list wastes the moment a reader is most engaged. Close with something concrete: a showing prompt, a note about competing interest if that's genuinely true, or the one detail you deliberately saved for last because it's the strongest.
