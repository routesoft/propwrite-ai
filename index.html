MLS COPYWRITING

How to write an MLS description that actually gets clicks

Buyers scan MLS descriptions in seconds. Here's the structure, the words to cut, and the details that actually earn a showing request.

August 15, 2026 · 7 min read

Most MLS descriptions lose the buyer in the first sentence. Not because the property is wrong for them — because the writing gives them nothing to picture. "Welcome to this stunning home" tells a scrolling buyer exactly nothing they didn't already know from the thumbnail.

Buyers on Zillow and the MLS spend, on average, a handful of seconds deciding whether to open a listing before moving to the next one. The description's job isn't to sell the house. It's to earn the click into the photos.

Lead with the detail that isn't in every other listing

Every listing in a price range has "updated kitchen" and "spacious backyard." Those aren't wrong, but they're not why this house gets a showing over the one three doors down. Open with whatever is actually specific: the year the roof was replaced, the direction the primary bedroom faces, the walk time to the elementary school, the fact that the kitchen was redone last spring and nothing has touched it since.

If a sentence could be pasted into any other listing on the same street without sounding wrong, it isn't doing any work.

Cut the words that have stopped meaning anything

"Cozy," "charming," and "must-see" have been used so often that buyers read past them without registering a single detail. They also carry unintended signals — "cozy" reads as "small" to a lot of buyers, whether or not that's what you meant. Replace the adjective with the fact that would have made a buyer believe it: not "cozy living room," but "living room fits a sectional and still leaves the doorway clear."

A quick swap list
  • Instead of "spacious" — give the square footage or a comparison ("primary bedroom fits a king bed with room for a reading chair")
  • Instead of "move-in ready" — name what was actually done and when ("new HVAC in 2025, repainted throughout in 2026")
  • Instead of "great for entertaining" — describe the layout that makes it true ("kitchen island seats four and opens directly to the patio")

Structure it the way a buyer actually reads

Buyers scan in a rough order: what is it, where is it, what's the standout, what's the catch (if any), what's next. A description that wanders — kitchen, then neighborhood, then back to the bathroom, then the school district — forces the reader to reassemble it themselves. Most won't bother.

A dependable order: one line on the property type and specs, two to three sentences on the two or three features that actually differentiate it, one line on location context that matters to the target buyer (commute, schools, walkability), and a short closing line that names the next step.

Match the length to the price point, not a template

A starter condo doesn't need six paragraphs, and a luxury estate undersold in eighty words looks like a mistake, not restraint. As a rough guide: entry-level and investment properties read well at 100–150 words, typical single-family homes at 150–220, and luxury or unusual properties can run to 250–300 when there's genuinely more to say. Past that, buyers stop reading before they finish.

Write the closing line like it's the only one that matters

It might be. A description that trails off after the feature list wastes the moment a reader is most engaged. Close with something concrete: a showing prompt, a note about competing interest if that's genuinely true, or the one detail you deliberately saved for last because it's the strongest.

Real estate social captions: a formula that isn't just emojis

A repeatable structure for Instagram and Facebook listing captions that stop the scroll without reading like a template.

August 19, 2026 · 6 min read

Open ten real estate Instagram accounts and you'll see the same caption shape nine times: a house emoji, "NEW LISTING," three bullet-pointed stats, a wall of hashtags. It's not wrong, exactly. It's just invisible — a pattern the algorithm and the eye have both learned to scroll past.

A caption's job on social is different from an MLS description's. Nobody follows you on Instagram to read specs; they follow to see what you're up to and whether they'd want you as their agent. The caption should sound like a person who's excited about this specific house, not a listing sheet with emojis sprinkled on top.

Start with a hook that isn't the address

"123 Maple Street is now available!" tells a scrolling follower nothing worth stopping for. A hook works when it's specific to this house and slightly conversational — the kind of thing you'd actually say to a friend. "The kitchen island in this one is bigger than my first apartment" stops a thumb. "New listing in Maple Heights" doesn't.

If the first line could caption any listing photo you've ever posted, it's not a hook — it's a placeholder.

A structure that holds up across property types

  • Line 1 — the hook: one sentence, specific to this house, written the way you'd talk
  • Line 2–3 — the feeling, then the fact: name what it's like to be in the space, then back it with one concrete detail (square footage, a renovation date, a view)
  • Line 4 — the practical bit: beds/baths, price if you share it, neighborhood
  • Line 5 — the call to action: DM, link in bio, or open house time — one, not three
  • Hashtags: a mix of neighborhood, city, and property-type tags, kept to 8–15 rather than maxed out

Emojis are seasoning, not the recipe

A house emoji next to every line reads as noise, and it's often the first thing that makes a caption look automated. Used well, an emoji marks one specific beat — the price, the open house date, the single feature you want to land. Used everywhere, none of them land.

A quick before/after

Before: "🏡 NEW LISTING 🏡 3 bed 2 bath in Austin! ✨ Updated kitchen 🔥 Huge backyard 🌳 DM for a showing! #realestate #newlisting #austinrealestate #dreamhome"

After: "The backyard on this one has room for a garden, a fire pit, and still space to throw a ball around. 3 bed, 2 bath, freshly updated kitchen, quiet street in South Austin. Open house Saturday 1–3, or DM to book a private tour."

Match the caption to the platform, not just the photo

Instagram captions can run a bit longer since the photo is doing first-impression work and the caption earns the second look. Facebook audiences (often more local, often older) respond better to a slightly more informative version — a few more concrete details, a little less slang. Same house, same structure, different dial on tone.

Writing five of these a week for five different listings is where the formula starts to feel like a chore. The buyer lead email that doesn't sound like a form letter

Why “Just checking in!” gets ignored, and what to send a new buyer lead in the first hour instead.

August 22, 2026 · 6 min read

A lead fills out a form at 9:14pm asking about a listing. By the time they get a reply, it's a subject line that says "Following Up" and a body that opens with "Hi, just checking in to see if you're still interested." They aren't rude enough to say it, but the honest answer is: less interested than they were nine minutes after they filled out the form.

Speed matters, but so does what actually lands in the inbox. A fast reply that reads like a form letter still reads like a form letter.

Why "just checking in" undersells the moment

The lead already told you what they're interested in — that's what the form was for. A reply that doesn't reference the property, the price range, or the reason they likely reached out wastes the one piece of context you were handed for free. It also signals, subtly, that this is an automated sequence rather than a person who read the inquiry.

The first email a lead gets is the only one guaranteed to be read closely. Every one after it competes with a fuller inbox and a cooler lead.

What the first-hour email needs to do

  • Reference the specific property or search criteria they inquired about — not "a listing," the actual one
  • Answer one likely question before they ask it — availability, whether it's still on the market, or a detail the listing photos didn't show
  • Offer one clear next step, not three. A single call-to-action outperforms a menu of options.
  • Sound like a person, not a brokerage. Short sentences, a real name, no "Dear Valued Client."

A structure that works across most first-touch emails

Open with the property or search, not a greeting. Add one sentence of context that shows you actually looked at what they asked about. Ask a single, easy-to-answer question that moves the conversation forward — a showing time, or whether they've been pre-approved. Close with your direct contact info, not just a signature block.

Short example

"Saw you're interested in the 3-bed on Maple — it's still active, and there's a showing slot open Thursday evening if that works for you. Happy to send over the disclosures ahead of time too. What's the best number to reach you at?"

That's four sentences. It references the property, answers the obvious first question (still available), gives one next step, and ends with something the lead can act on without composing a reply.

The follow-up sequence matters more than any single email

Most leads don't convert on the first message regardless of how well it's written. What separates agents who convert leads over time isn't a better opening line — it's a second and third touch that keeps adding something new (a price change, a similar listing, an answer to a question they raised) instead of repeating "just checking in" on a loop.

What to say when every house has a “chef's kitchen”

Zillow descriptions live or die on differentiation. Here's how to describe a kitchen, a yard, or a neighborhood that doesn't sound like every other one.

August 26, 2026 · 6 min read

Search "chef's kitchen" on Zillow in almost any mid-size city and the results run into the thousands. It's not that every one of those kitchens is lying — plenty genuinely have a six-burner range and a big island. It's that the phrase has been used so often it no longer tells a buyer anything distinguishing. It's become wallpaper.

The same thing has happened to a short list of other phrases: "entertainer's dream," "resort-style backyard," "prime location." Each one started as a real description of a real feature and has been worn smooth by repetition. The fix isn't to avoid the feature — it's to describe the specific version of it that's actually in this house.

Replace the label with the evidence

A buyer trusts a fact more than an adjective, because a fact can be checked against the photos. Instead of naming the category ("chef's kitchen"), name the thing that would make a buyer conclude it themselves.

Overused phrase Try instead
Chef's kitchen "Six-burner gas range, waterfall-edge island, pantry big enough to hide the mixer"
Resort-style backyard "Pool with a baja shelf, outdoor kitchen under a covered patio, no rear neighbors"
Prime location "Six-minute walk to the Elm Street Caltrain stop, two blocks from the Saturday farmers market"
Entertainer's dream "Open kitchen-to-living flow, bar seating for six, sliding doors to a covered deck"

Zillow rewards descriptions that match what the photos show

Buyers on Zillow flip through photos and read the description almost simultaneously, often on a phone, often comparing to four other tabs. A description that oversells relative to the photos creates a small, immediate letdown — and buyers remember which listings did that. A description that names exactly what's visible builds a bit of trust that carries into the showing request.

If a phrase would survive being pasted into a listing with completely different photos, it isn't describing this house.

Don't over-correct into a spec sheet

The goal isn't to strip out all feeling and list dimensions like an inspection report. It's to earn the feeling with a fact instead of asserting it with an adjective. "Morning light floods the kitchen through the east-facing bay window" does more work than either "sunny kitchen" (vague) or "kitchen has a bay window" (flat) alone — it keeps the feeling and grounds it in something real.

Build a short list of house-specific facts before you write a word

Before drafting, jot five or six details that are true of this house and would be false of the listing next door: an exact appliance brand, a renovation year, a specific view, a distance to something buyers in this area actually care about. Those five or six facts are the raw material — the description is just arranging them so a buyer keeps reading.
